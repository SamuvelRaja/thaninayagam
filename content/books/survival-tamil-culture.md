## The Survival of Tamil Culture

Xavier S. Thani Nayagam (as Editor) · *Tamil Culture*, Vol. I No. 2 (June 1952), pp. 99–103

THE DISINTEGRATION and disappearance of dominant cultures is one of the most painful observations in the study of history.

> They say the Lion and the Lizard keep  
> The Courts where Jamshyd gloried and drank deep.

A sense of sadness and regret comes over the student of culture that walks among the ruins of Sumer or Akkad or Crete or Athens, and realises that men could have altered the course of history. The disappearance of cultures is not explained by a blind and predetermined cyclic process as Otto Spengler attempted to prove in his book on the *Decline of the West*. While it is true that a culture may not be produced artificially or developed according to a pre-conceived plan, it is no less true that the rational and spiritual elements in a culture are those that determine its creative activity, and these are contributed by the mind of man. It is in the power of men to contribute the causes and work at those conditions necessary for a flowering of culture, and it is also in the power of men to combat those intellectual errors and the emotional prejudices which stand in the way of such conditions. The survival as well as the growth of Tamil Culture is, therefore, in our own hands.

There need be no fear that the growth of Indian or Ceylonese national unity, or even the spread of a world culture, threatens the survival of the culture of the Tamils. If the culture of India and Ceylon is to have an organic and natural development in the future, it will be in the interest of these countries to promote a culture which has been basic and is at the roots of their history, and to foster the language and the literature that has been responsible for the preservation of that culture. If the growth of separate regional cultures in Wales, in Scotland, in Ireland have been argued to be necessary for the development of English Culture, with how much more justification may it not be stated that any attempt to interfere with the language, the literature, and the way of life of the millions of Tamils or to weaken their culture by questionable politico-economic methods would spell ruin for that wealth, vitality and diversity which have been characteristic of culture in our parts of the world.¹

From that dawn of history when Dravidian religious, ethical, and material elements entered Vedic and post-Vedic civilization and made a cultural conquest not unlike the classical one related by Horace,

> *Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit et artes*

to the present day when Tamil artisans from Tanjore create beauty with stone and chisel at the Dalada Maligawa and the edifices of the Ceylon University, the Tamil contribution to other cultures has been consistently considerable. Since a healthy diversity is necessary in a unity for the development of culture, any attempt in India or Ceylon to impose the tyranny of one language, one outlook, one way of life would be tantamount to the negation of culture, and would result in the failure that like experiments have encountered in our generation in Italy and Germany. Even in a country like Malaya where the Tamil-speaking people form a minority, the conservation of Tamil culture will strengthen the future development of the Federation. It is this belief that has made the University of Malaya organize a department of Tamil Studies and South Indian culture. It is the unimaginative statesman that aims at the tyranny of a rigid uniformity and embraces the view that any culture incompatible with his own should be forcibly uprooted.

Nor is it reasonable to fear that dominant cultures like Tamil, and *a fortiori* satellite cultures, would cease to exist before the advance of a future world culture. T. S. Eliot has stated very clearly what the reaction of a student and lover of culture would be to the evolution of a world culture such as is envisaged by the promoters of a world state and a world government. “We can only conceive it (world culture) as the logical term of relations between cultures… It must follow from what I have already pleaded about the value of local cultures, that a world culture which was simply a uniform culture would be no culture at all. We should have a humanity de-humanised. It would be a nightmare.”²

Tamil culture far from being absorbed in a world culture would receive from it opportunities for growth and for service. The United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organisation has thus defined its objectives:

1. To develop and maintain mutual understanding and appreciation of the life and culture, the arts, the humanities and the sciences of the peoples of the world, as a basis for effective international organization and world peace.
2. To co-operate in extending and in making available to all peoples for the service of common human needs the world’s full body of knowledge and culture, and in assuring its contribution to the economic stability, political security, and general well-being of the peoples of the world.

The activities of the international body have been said to be vague and impractical, and such observations are probably due to the new ideas that the organisation has introduced in the educational and cultural field. It is certainly giving an orientation unemphasised before. Ananda Coomaraswamy realised the need to establish world unity and understanding on a knowledge of the culture, literature, and the spiritual achievements of peoples, and he advocated that norms of conduct should be taken not only from Greek and Latin classics, but also from Tamil, from Arabic, from Persian and from Chinese. The Unesco up to now has not turned its thoughts to Tamil, but we fervently hope it will. The lover of Tamil culture is not concerned so much about a possible weakening or inadequate recognition of Tamil culture, as about the world neglecting or losing a most precious heritage.

The manner in which Tamil culture has resisted all attempts at being absorbed by alien cultures argues an inherent vitality which strikes the student of culture as a most remarkable phenomenon. By the usual laws of change known to history there were periods when the Tamils and their culture should have been so absorbed as to have lost their identity; when their language should have been so changed as to have lost its original structure, and should have been so Sanscritized as to create today controversies regarding its origins and its classification. The advent of the Aryans, the activities of the Jains and the Buddhists, the patronage of exclusive classes by Tamil kings, an exaggerated appreciation and imitation of foreign styles and modes, the conquest of the Tamil country by European nations, all these events brought in their wake a number of trends unfavourable to the organic growth of the culture of the Tamils. But every period of danger was marked by a reaction in favour of maintaining its purity and identity, and thus has Tamil Society reached its present state with its own system of values and its own cultural ethos.

In the relations between a given culture and contemporaneous cultures, there are always two forces of action, the force of attraction and the force of repulsion. Especially in our age is contact with other cultures a source of strength and inspiration. Even those influences mentioned above which occasioned a struggle for survival, eventually served to enrich Tamil culture because Tamil society had the vitality to react in time and assimilate foreign elements of worth into its organism without any danger to its identity. The force of repulsion too, paradoxically enough, is one of the most creative forces in the realm of culture. It is friction that produces the spark, and it is danger that augments the instinct of self-preservation. There are enough causes today for Tamil Society to be constantly vigilant, and vigilance is as much the price of culture as of liberty.

When we compare the state of the Tamil language and literature and Tamil way of life fifty years ago with their present state, we see many encouraging signs to show that we are witnessing the beginnings of a great revival. It has been usual to attribute this revival to the great patriot and poet, Subramania Bharathi. While we are willing to ascribe to him the chief influence in the reawakening of a national and political consciousness among the Tamils, and new trends in Tamil poetry, the literary and cultural revival, we feel, should be traced to another source.

Just as the popularization of the classics of Greece and Rome and the study of the origins of European Culture occasioned the Renaissance, so too the popularization through print of the Cangam works and the classical epics of the subsequent epochs are responsible for the new life that is manifest in Tamil letters, and the new interest shown in Tamiliana. To no single man would it be possible to attribute the beginnings of this Renaissance. It is a movement towards the success of which many outstanding men have contributed their share; religious revivalists like Ramalinga Adigal, Arumuga Navalar, and Vedanayagam Pillai; scholars like Meenakshisundaram Pillai, Sundaram Pillai and V. P. Subramania Mudaliyar; editors like C. Y. Thamotharam Pillai and Swaminatha Aiyer; writers like Maraimalai Adigal and Thiru V. Kalyanasundara Mudaliar; interpreters in English like Kanagasabai Pillai and Dr. Pope. This movement has created a deep interest in the roots and origins of Tamil Culture; it has been responsible for the eschewing of superfluous words and phrases of Sanscrit origin which were introduced in Tamil vocabulary during periods when Sanscrit was considered “the language of the gods”, and Tamil thought to be an inadequate medium of expression. This movement has caused a revival of interest in Tamil Music, in Tamil dances and in Tamil drama. In brief, it has made the Tamil conscious of his past, and that is the happiest augury for the future since the future should be well rooted in the past.

In the realm of poetry, this is Bharathi’s age, but little of genuine poetry has been produced since his death. Tamil prose reveals the signs of conscious attempts by writers to make it the medium of expression of modern thought and literary criticism. The output has been prolific in certain sections of Tamil prose, but depth, vigour, strength and character have not been its noteworthy features. Arthur Clutton-Brock said some years ago “The English Plato is yet to be.” So too the Tamil Newman, the Tamil Milton, the Tamil Cronin, and even the Tamil Tagore are yet to be.

The survival as well as the positive growth of Tamil Culture is happily based on the study of the past of the Tamils. The present revival is bound to be yet fruitful in proportion to the study that the Tamils themselves are able to make of their own language, their own literature and their own origins. For the maintenance as well as for the transmission of a culture there is no safeguard more reliable than language, and no means better suited than the family. It is in the family that the earliest and also the permanent attitude towards a culture is developed. Hence in bi-lingual areas an effort should be made in Tamil homes to teach the mother-tongue in all its purity, and inculcate the finer and nobler elements of the Tamil heritage.³

The many Tamil festivals, lectures and conferences that are becoming popular in the Tamil country are promoting a very welcome enthusiasm. But enthusiasm can be short-lived unless it springs from intellectual convictions. Such convictions are formed by study, by the cultural climate in the family, and by an understanding of that “perfection”, and that “sweetness and light” that culture bestows on the individual and on society. It is because of persons endowed with such “perfection” that life is livable. To quote the Tamil sage:

> பண்புடையார்ப் பட்டுண்டு உலகம் அது இன்றேல்  
> மண்புக்கு மாய்வது மண்

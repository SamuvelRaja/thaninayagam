Tamil poetry abounds with passages in which
the fertility of a region or kingdom, unfailmg rains,
well-watered rivers, abundance of birds and beasts
and general prosperity are ascribed to the righteous
tule of the sovereign. The panegyric poems of the
Pattuppattu, the Patirruppaitu and the Purananuru
are impregnated with this idea. The praise ofa sove-
reign’s rule meant the praise of the hills, of the rivers
and the general beauty and fertility of his kingdom.!
Sometimes the panegyrics assume a language re-
miniscent of Vergil in the fourth eclogue. Des-
cribmg the region of Ilantirayan, the poet says,
“in his land there are no marauders that attack way-
farers to rob them of their goods. Peals of thunder
you will not hear; the serpent will not kill, nor the
forest animals hurt. Proceed, therefore, as you
will.’’?

When praising a sovereign, the roar of the ocean
or the cataract, and the sound of thunder, are said
to be the drums that announce his greatness. The
heights of the mountains bespeak the heights of great-
ness achieved by him. The streams that descend
the mountain sides may be seen shimmering silver-
like from far and wide; they are the silver banners
that proclaim his victories and his unrivalled prowess
in war.. The waters even in the swiftest and
deepest of his rivers are so clear that ornaments that
have fallen off from those sporting in the river may be -
seen lying deep down on the sands. The clear waters
are an indication of the unsullied record of his rule.*

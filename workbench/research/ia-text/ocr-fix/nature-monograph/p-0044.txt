Since the earliest Tamil poetry that has come
down to us is the product of a highly civilized period
and the result of centuries of a well-established cul-
ture, it is not surprising that its interpretation of
Nature does not contain the simple or the utilitarian
appreciation as the prominent characteristic. When
the Cangam classics came into being, Tamil poetry
had already for many epochs reached the last and
final stage of its evolution in the poetic appreciation
of Nature. Lines do occur which express simple
joy at the coolness of water, the pleasures of shade
and bower, child-like joy at the sight or the use of
flowers; lines do occur when the material beauty
of the universe is expressed with the freshness of
Keats and the keen observation of a Vergil or Ten-
nyson, but these lines are never without relation to
man. However, in Tamil poetry occur certain con-
ventions which are the remains and echoes of a very
primitive and distant age when there was less faith
in the interaction of Nature and humanity though
there was more of the immediate contact with Nature.

In order to assess this Tamil humanism one has to
consider the entire complex of attitudes prevalent and
fostered in Tamil society, and the abstractions and univer-
sals which comprise the conscious and unconscious
metaphysic of the age as expressed in the anthologies. The
love poetry and the panegyric poetry show the range of
psychological insight of the poets themselves and the
Tolkappiyam is probably derived from one of the several
compendia which existed as aids to the study of the phone-
tics, grammar, prosody and the conventions and psycho-
logy which were to guide poetic compositions. Life is
considered pleasant and joyful, and death a sad ending ;
there is no attempt to obtain release from life except to
vindicate honour and bravery and the love for a husband
who has passed away ; there is a note of nostalgic sadness
in the poems about senescence and old age; liberality and
ethical goodness are indulged in without motives of expia-
tion and penance; rebirth is seldom spoken of in the
earlier poems but the life after death is represented as an
abode of permanent happiness for the good and permanent
suffering for the wicked ; happiness in the future world
is conceded only to those who by their bravery and altru-
ism-have established their glory in this world ;** love and
courtship, marriage and children are considered necessary
modes of human fulfilment and perfection of personality ;
and the norms and ideals of statecraft, ethics, truth, justice,
equity, gratitude and love which run through the antho-
logies and the Tirukkural seem to make by comparison
Kautilya, Manu and Vatsyayana small, exclusive and
petty in the ideals and concepts they teach of moral and
ethical goodness.*

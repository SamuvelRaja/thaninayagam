or two to India and China, they seem to do so to satisfy
some qualm of historical conscience or much in the fashion
of a sop thrown to an intellectual and cultural Cerberus.’
The student of Comparative Education, therefore, has
a vast and unexplored field before him, and it is yet possible
for him to work as a pioneer in synthesising the world’s
experience and cultures at given epochs and at given
periods of her history. The world is not understood and
the achievements of mankind are not understood except
“by an appreciation of the intangible, impalpable, spiritual
and cultural forces” operating in various parts of the world
at given epochs. It comes naturally to persons familiar
with Eastern and Western thought to desire that standard
works on the History of Secondary Education and the
History of Universities and reference books on educational.
